<section id="about" class="syllabus-section">
  <h2>About this course</h2>
  <p>Introduces intelligent vehicles and autonomous driving systems, emphasizing <strong>multimodal perception, prediction, and motion planning</strong> under real-world constraints &mdash; both for the external driving scene and the in-cabin observation of drivers and passengers.</p>
  <p>You'll work with camera, lidar, radar, and audio sensing; learning-based scene understanding; behavior and trajectory forecasting; the fundamentals of localization, mapping, and control; and in-cabin monitoring and interaction. Evaluation is grounded in <strong>public driving datasets and simulation</strong>, with a running theme of safety, human interaction, ethics, and responsible deployment.</p>

  <h3>Conjoined UG / graduate offering</h3>
  <p>CSE 164 and EECS 264 share the same lectures and topic structure. Where the versions differ:</p>
  <ul class="clean-list">
    <li><strong>Undergraduate (CSE 164):</strong> paired paper presentations, team-based project reimplementing or adapting existing methods with clear experimental validation, mastery of established techniques.</li>
    <li><strong>Graduate (EECS 264):</strong> individual paper presentations with critical evaluation of assumptions and limitations; individual or team project requiring a novel or extended technical contribution beyond existing baselines, culminating in a final presentation or demo.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Prerequisites</h3>
  <p><strong>Required:</strong> CSE 100 and MATH 024.<br>
  <strong>Strongly recommended:</strong> linear algebra, probability/statistics, Python, and machine learning fundamentals. If you're rusty on any of these, that's okay &mdash; come to office hours and we'll point you to refreshers.</p>
</section>

<section id="project" class="syllabus-section">
  <h2>Semester project</h2>
  <p>The heart of the course. Each team designs and executes an empirical study using real autonomous-driving data or simulation. The project runs the length of the semester &mdash; introduced in the early weeks, refined through a milestone check-in, and delivered as a final report.</p>

  <h3>What you'll do</h3>
  <ul class="clean-list">
    <li><strong>Hypothesis:</strong> submit a written proposal with a testable question (e.g., how a particular method or model choice affects perception or driving performance).</li>
    <li><strong>Experimental design:</strong> pick datasets or simulators, define evaluation metrics, and justify controls and comparisons.</li>
    <li><strong>Data &amp; analysis:</strong> run models on public datasets (nuScenes, Argoverse, CARLA, nuPlan) or collect/annotate your own data; compute quantitative results (detection accuracy, trajectory error, failure rates).</li>
    <li><strong>Interpretation:</strong> analyze uncertainty, domain shift, and failure cases.</li>
    <li><strong>Revision:</strong> refine methods after the milestone review.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Undergraduate vs. graduate expectations</h3>
  <ul class="clean-list">
    <li><strong>Undergraduate (CSE 164):</strong> team-based project reimplementing or adapting existing methods, with correct frameworks, experimental validation, and clear documentation of task performance.</li>
    <li><strong>Graduate (EECS 264):</strong> individual or team-based project requiring a novel or extended technical contribution beyond existing baselines &mdash; literature positioning, controlled experiments, and discussion of limitations. Suitable for continued research. Final presentation or demo required.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Ethical impact section</h3>
  <p>Every project must include a required <strong>Ethical Impact Section</strong> analyzing potential bias in your data or models, safety and failure modes, privacy and human-interaction implications, and consequences of real-world deployment.</p>
</section>

<section id="presentations" class="syllabus-section">
  <h2>Paper presentations</h2>
  <p>Each student (or pair, for undergraduates) presents one peer-reviewed paper from the reading list.</p>
  <ul class="clean-list">
    <li>State the research question or hypothesis, the experimental design, the evaluation methodology, and sources of uncertainty.</li>
    <li>Lead the class discussion &mdash; be prepared to defend or critique the paper's scientific validity.</li>
    <li><strong>Graduate presenters</strong> present individually and are expected to include critical evaluation of assumptions, limitations, and research implications.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section id="outcomes" class="syllabus-section">
  <h2>Course learning outcomes</h2>
  <ol class="clean-list">
    <li><strong>Systems thinking.</strong> Explain the autonomous driving stack (sensing &rarr; perception &rarr; prediction &rarr; planning/control) and evaluate design tradeoffs across components.</li>
    <li><strong>Implementation.</strong> Implement, assess, and present core perception, forecasting, or planning components using public datasets and standard evaluation metrics.</li>
    <li><strong>Multimodal representations.</strong> Understand, apply, critically assess, and communicate multimodal learning concepts &mdash; fusion across vision, lidar, radar, and audio, and representation learning for spatiotemporal data.</li>
    <li><strong>Classical + data-driven models.</strong> Understand, implement, and critically evaluate classical and data-driven models for autonomous driving tasks.</li>
    <li><strong>Failure &amp; safety analysis.</strong> Analyze failure modes, dataset bias, safety constraints, and human-interaction considerations relevant to intelligent vehicles.</li>
  </ol>
</section>

<section id="policies" class="syllabus-section">
  <h2>Policies</h2>

  <details class="policy">
    <summary><strong>Academic integrity</strong></summary>
    <p>Academic honesty is taken very seriously at UC Merced. <strong>Talk to each other about concepts &mdash; that's how you learn.</strong> But when it's time to turn in work, write it yourself.</p>
    <p>Not allowed:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Sharing or receiving solutions before, during, or after the semester.</li>
      <li>Submitting a friend's work.</li>
      <li>Examining someone else's solution for "ideas."</li>
      <li>Submitting ChatGPT / GitHub Copilot output as your own solution.</li>
      <li>Posting course solutions or materials publicly.</li>
      <li>Code obfuscation to dodge detection.</li>
    </ul>
    <p><strong>Penalties:</strong> first offense &rarr; the negative of the assignment's maximum points (and it cannot be dropped). Second offense &rarr; automatic F in the course. All cases are reported to the Office of Student Rights and Responsibilities.</p>

  <p class="muted">Click a question to expand.</p>

  <details class="faq"><summary>Should I take CSE 164 or EECS 264?</summary><p>CSE 164 is the undergraduate offering; EECS 264 is the conjoined graduate version. Grad students extend this with a novel contribution and a final presentation/demo. Every project includes a required Ethical Impact Section.</p></details>

  <details class="faq"><summary>Does this satisfy a GE requirement?</summary><p>Yes &mdash; the course is designed to meet the <strong>Ethics</strong> and <strong>Scientific Method</strong> Intellectual Experience GE designations.</p></details>

  <details class="faq"><summary>Is there an Honors version?</summary><p>Yes. Honors students give an individual paper presentation, complete a project with an explicit novel research component, and are held to elevated standards in scholarly engagement, analytical depth, and project rigor &mdash; bridging the undergraduate and graduate versions of the course.</p></details>
